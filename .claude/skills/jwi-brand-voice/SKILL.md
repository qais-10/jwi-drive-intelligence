# JWI Brand Voice

JWI is an independent creative agency with international experience and a strong understanding of the Middle East. This skill turns a brief, draft, idea, or source material into communication that sounds distinctly like JWI — not like a generic agency — and is ready to publish.

Work through the steps in order. Steps 1–3 are diagnostic (understand before writing); steps 4–8 are the actual writing/editing; steps 9–10 are the finishing pass. Don't skip straight to polishing sentences before you know what the piece is trying to do and who it's for.

## 1. Understand the brief

Before writing a word, be clear on:
- **Goal** — what is this communication meant to achieve?
- **Audience** — who is actually reading this?
- **Channel** — website, deck, proposal, social post, PR, email, internal comms? Each has a different register and length.
- **Action** — what should the reader think, feel, or do after reading it?

If the user hasn't given you these, ask, or infer them clearly from context and state your assumption so it can be corrected.

## 2. Find the one idea

Every piece needs a single strongest central point — not just polished sentences strung together. Before drafting, identify: *what is the one thing this piece is actually saying?* If you can't state it in one sentence, the piece doesn't have a point yet, and no amount of line-editing will fix that. Get the idea right first, then write to it.

## 3. Ground it in real JWI material — never invent claims

Check the repo and the conversation for JWI-approved source material: company profile, website copy, case studies, portfolio, PR coverage, brand philosophy, the JWI Standard, or anything else the user has shared. Use it as the evidence base.

**Never invent clients, results, awards, capabilities, or claims that aren't in the source material or given to you by the user.** If you don't have evidence for a specific claim (a client name, a metric, an award), don't fabricate one — either ask the user for the real detail, write the point in a way that doesn't require unverifiable specifics, or flag clearly that a placeholder needs to be filled in with real information before publishing. This matters more than sounding impressive: an accurate, slightly plainer sentence beats a vivid, made-up one.

## 4. Write from JWI's actual position

JWI writes as an independent creative agency with international experience and a strong understanding of the Middle East. That's a real, specific vantage point — not generic "global agency" boilerplate. Let it show through the angle you take, not through repeating the phrase itself.

## 5. Calibrate the voice

JWI's voice sits at specific points on several tensions — not just "professional and friendly." Hold both sides of each pair:

| Yes | Not |
|---|---|
| Confident | Arrogant |
| Intelligent | Academic |
| Creative | Unclear |
| Bold | Exaggerated |
| Human, direct, conversational | Stiff, corporate |
| Culturally aware, globally minded | Parochial or tone-deaf |
| Concise, purposeful, commercially relevant | Padded, decorative |

A quick test: if a line only satisfies the left column by sacrificing the right (e.g., "bold" tipping into "exaggerated"), it's off-voice — pull it back rather than cutting the boldness.

## 6. Lead with the point

Open with the most compelling idea or benefit, not a warm-up. Cut slow introductions, throat-clearing context, and predictable agency openers ("In a world where...", "At JWI, we believe..."). The reader should get the point in the first line, not after three sentences of scene-setting.

## 7. Write clean, intentional sentences

- Strong verbs over weak verb + noun constructions ("we grew the brand," not "we drove growth for the brand").
- Vary sentence length deliberately — a short sentence after a longer one creates emphasis; don't let everything run at the same rhythm.
- Plain English over jargon. If a simpler word says the same thing, use it.
- Every sentence should be doing something. If a sentence just restates the previous one with more adjectives, cut it.

## 8. Tie creativity to an outcome

Don't present creative thinking as decoration or present ideas or descriptions of the work without stating what the thinking achieved for the business, brand, or audience. When you describe a strategy, an idea, or a piece of work, say what it did — changed perception, drove a specific behavior, solved a specific problem — using evidence from the source material where available (see step 3). If a project example is available, use it in place of generic marketing advice; specificity is more persuasive than a general claim about "creativity" or "strategy."

## 9. Cut clichés and agency-speak

Strike phrases like these — and anything in the same register — on sight:

- "We bring ideas to life"
- "We push boundaries"
- "In today's fast-paced world"
- "One-stop shop"
- "Cutting-edge solutions"
- "Results-driven creativity"
- "360-degree agency"
- "We are passionate about…"

**Illustrative example (not real JWI copy):**
> Before: *"In today's fast-paced world, we bring bold ideas to life through results-driven creativity."*
> After: *"We turn a hard business problem into work people actually notice — and act on."*

The "after" version isn't a template to reuse verbatim; it's there to show the shift from empty agency phrasing to a specific, concrete claim. Every real piece needs its own specific point, grounded in step 3's source material.

## 10. Name check — never skip this

Before finalizing, scan the piece specifically for the agency name. It must read **JWI**, never **JWI Global**, with one exception: quoting a legal name, a legacy source, or a URL that literally cannot be changed (and in that case, it should be obvious from context why the fuller name appears). This is a fast, mechanical check — do it as a dedicated last pass, not just as something you'd probably catch while reading for tone.

## 11. Final review

Before handing back the finished piece, check it against this list. If any answer is "no," revise before delivering:

- [ ] Does this sound recognisably like JWI, not a generic agency?
- [ ] Is the main point clear immediately, not buried?
- [ ] Is every claim accurate and backed by real source material or user-provided information?
- [ ] Is it free of the clichés and agency-speak listed in step 9?
- [ ] Is it appropriate for the stated audience and channel?
- [ ] Can anything be cut without losing meaning?
- [ ] Does it leave the reader with a clear impression or a clear next step?
- [ ] Does it say JWI, not JWI Global, throughout (barring a genuine legal/legacy exception)?

Deliver the piece once it passes all eight. If you had to leave a placeholder for a claim you couldn't verify (per step 3), flag it explicitly to the user rather than letting it pass silently.
